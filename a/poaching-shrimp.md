Boiling shrimp overcooks them. Water that has just come off the boil holds enough heat to cook them through and nothing more, so the variable to control is not time on the flame — it is how long they sit in a covered pot with the heat off.

## Why the Heat Goes Off

Shrimp are small, thin, and almost all muscle. A 21–25 count shrimp is maybe 15mm through the thickest part of the tail, and its proteins set between 120 and 140°F. In water at a rolling boil the outside races past that while the centre catches up, and the fibres contract hard enough to squeeze out moisture — which is what makes a boiled shrimp rubbery and curled into a tight O.

3800g of water off the boil is a large heat reservoir cooling slowly. Drop in 450g of cold shrimp and the pot settles somewhere around 160–170°F, then drifts down. That is above the setting point and below the temperature where the muscle wrings itself out, and it stays in that band for several minutes. The shrimp cook through with the fibres relaxed.

Two things make this fail:

- **Not enough water.** Below about 2800g per 450g of shrimp the pot drops under 150°F when they go in, and they stew in cooling water instead of poaching. More water is a bigger reservoir and a more forgiving one.
- **The lid off.** The trapped heat is doing the cooking. An uncovered pot sheds it into the room, and the times below are all wrong.

## How Long, by Size and Shell

Rows are the count per pound — the number printed on the bag, which is the only honest measure of shrimp size. Columns are what is still attached. Minutes off heat, covered, for 450–700g of shrimp in 3800g of liquid.

| Count per lb | Peeled | Shell on | Head + shell |
|---|---|---|---|
| 41–50 small | 2–3 | 3–4 | — |
| 31–40 medium | 2–3 | 3–4 | — |
| 26–30 large | 3–4 | 4–5 | 5–6 |
| 21–25 extra large | 3–4 | 4–5 | 5–7 |
| 16–20 jumbo | 4–5 | 5–6 | 6–8 |
| U-15 colossal | 5–6 | 7–9 | 8–10 |

Read across a row rather than down a column. The shell is a barrier, and a head is both a barrier and extra mass to heat — which is why the third column runs longest and why small shrimp are rarely sold head-on.

These are a starting point, not stopwatch measurements. The test is the shrimp: cut one open at the low end of its range and look. Opaque all the way through, and bent into a C rather than curled into a closed O. Underdone gets 60 more seconds with the lid back on, which costs nothing. Overdone cannot be undone.

Add about a minute if the shrimp went in straight from the fridge, or if they were frozen and thawed and still cold at the centre. Subtract about a minute if they sat out 20 minutes first.

## The Ice Bath Is Salted

Draining hot shrimp onto a plate leaves them cooking in their own heat for another minute or two, which is enough to lose what the gentle poach bought. They go into ice water, and the ice water gets salt in it.

Plain water is a hypotonic solution against seasoned shrimp: it pulls salt and soluble flavour out through the surface. A fistful of salt in the bath stops that gradient. Three minutes is enough to arrest the cooking; longer waterlogs them.

Then drain on a rack, and chill uncovered for an hour before dressing them. A dry surface takes sauce; a wet one dilutes it.

## Shells Are the Second Ingredient

Shrimp shells carry most of the flavour that makes a shrimp taste like the sea — glutamates, glycine, and the carotenoids that turn the broth orange. Cooking shell-on gets some of that into the flesh, but the real return is the poaching liquid.

Strain it, simmer the peeled shells in it for 15 minutes, strain again, and chill. That is *caldo de camarón*, and it is the difference between a Mexican shrimp cocktail that tastes of shrimp and one that tastes of ketchup. Buying peeled shrimp to save ten minutes of work costs you the broth.

The liquid is worth building even when nothing needs the caldo: 3800g water, 45g kosher salt, a halved lemon, a bay leaf, 2g peppercorns, and a few parsley stems, boiled hard for 5 minutes before the shrimp go anywhere near it. Salt the water like pasta water — the shrimp season from the outside in and there is no second chance once they are cooked.
